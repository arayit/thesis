\section{Photoacoustic Spectroscopy}
\label{sec:pas}

Photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) is an absorption-based detection technique in
which the \emph{absorbed} optical power --- rather than the transmitted power ---
is measured, through the sound that the absorption generates. The effect was
first reported by Bell in 1880, who found that a periodically interrupted beam of
sunlight falling on a sample produced an audible tone~\cite{Bell1880}. It
remained a curiosity until intense, spectrally pure laser sources turned it into
a practical method for trace-gas analysis~\needcite{review establishing modern
laser-based photoacoustic spectroscopy for trace-gas sensing}.

The mechanism is a short chain of energy conversion. When the laser wavelength
coincides with a molecular absorption line, analyte molecules are promoted to an
excited rovibrational state. At the pressures of interest here they relax
predominantly through non-radiative collisions (vibrational-to-translational, or
V--T, relaxation), converting the absorbed energy into local heating of the
gas~\needcite{non-radiative (V--T) relaxation as the origin of the photoacoustic
signal}. If the incident optical power is modulated at a frequency $f$, the
heating --- and therefore the local pressure --- is modulated at the same
frequency, launching an acoustic wave that a suitable transducer detects.

For an optically thin sample the absorbed power is $P_\text{abs}=P_0\,\alpha L$,
where $P_0$ is the incident optical power, $\alpha$ the absorption coefficient,
and $L$ an interaction length. The absorption coefficient is itself proportional
to the analyte number density, $\alpha=\sigma N$, with $\sigma$ the absorption
cross-section. The photoacoustic signal $S$ is therefore proportional to the
product of laser power and analyte concentration,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pas-signal}
  S \;=\; K\,P_0\,\alpha \;=\; K\,P_0\,\sigma N,
\end{equation}
where the cell constant $K$ collects the transducer responsivity and the cell
geometry~\needcite{standard photoacoustic signal expression / definition of the
cell constant}. Two consequences of \cref{eq:pas-signal} matter throughout this
thesis. First, the technique is intrinsically \emph{zero-background}: with no
absorption there is no signal, so sensitivity improves simply by raising the
laser power, unlike a transmission measurement that must resolve a small change
against a large background. Second, because $S\propto N$, calibrating it against
known concentrations gives a direct quantitative measurement route, used in
\cref{ch:results}.

Conventionally the transducer is a sensitive microphone in a gas cell whose
dimensions form an acoustic resonator at $f$, amplifying the
signal~\needcite{resonant photoacoustic cell with microphone detection}. This
approach is limited by the microphone's susceptibility to environmental acoustic
noise and by the relatively large cell volume it requires. The quartz-enhanced
variant described next removes both limitations by replacing the microphone with
a sharply resonant quartz tuning fork.

\section{Detection Scheme: Amplitude Modulation with a Pulsed QCL}
\label{sec:detection}

For a continuous-wave laser, wavelength modulation with second-harmonic (2f)
lock-in detection is common. With a \emph{pulsed} QCL, as used in this thesis,
the laser intensity is instead modulated at the tuning-fork resonance $f_0$
(amplitude modulation), and the photoacoustic signal is recovered with a lock-in
amplifier at the fundamental (1f). The absorption line is scanned by slowly
tuning the laser wavelength via temperature and/or drive current. Explain the
trade-offs of pulsed operation (e.g.\ spectral broadening, duty cycle) relative
to CW.

\section{Spectroscopy of Nitrous Oxide}
\label{sec:n2o-spectroscopy}

\nto\ has its strongest fundamental ($\nu_3$) near \SI{4.5}{\micro\meter}, but the
tunable quantum cascade laser used in this work (Daylight Solutions MIRcat-2300)
covers \SIrange{1111}{2000}{\per\centi\meter} (\SIrange{5}{9}{\micro\meter}).
The accessible \nto\ feature is therefore the $\nu_1$ fundamental band near
\SI{7.7}{\micro\meter}; the line used here is at \SI{1297.8}{\per\centi\meter},
with a secondary line at \SI{1271.5}{\per\centi\meter}. Line selection is made
with the HITRAN database~\cite{hitran2020}. A key practical issue in this region
is strong interference from water vapour (\water), which has many lines across
the \SIrange{1111}{2000}{\per\centi\meter} window (demonstrated in
\cref{ch:results}). Nitrogen, used as the buffer gas, is essentially
non-absorbing here.

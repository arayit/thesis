\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{ch:background}

This chapter gives the background needed to follow the rest of the thesis. It introduces the principle of photoacoustic spectroscopy, the QEPAS technique and its transducer, the detection scheme used with a pulsed laser, the figures of merit used to compare sensors, and the spectroscopy of \nto.

\section{Photoacoustic Spectroscopy}
\label{sec:pas}

Photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) is an absorption-based detection technique that measures the absorbed optical power rather than the transmitted power, by sensing the sound that the absorption generates. The effect was first reported by Bell in 1880, who found that a periodically interrupted beam of sunlight falling on a sample produced an audible tone~\cite{Bell1880}. It stayed a laboratory curiosity until intense and spectrally pure laser sources turned it into a practical method for trace-gas analysis~\cite{miklos2001}.

The mechanism is a short chain of energy conversion. When the laser wavelength coincides with a molecular absorption line, the analyte absorbs the light and releases that energy as heat through molecular collisions. If the incident optical power is modulated at a frequency $f$, the heating and therefore the local pressure are modulated at the same frequency, which launches an acoustic wave that a suitable transducer can detect. The strength of this signal depends on how quickly the absorbed energy turns into heat, so the molecular relaxation has to be fast compared with the modulation period. This holds for most molecules at the pressures used here, but the signal can weaken at low pressure or for species that relax slowly~\needcite{vibrational-translational relaxation and its effect on the photoacoustic signal}.

For an optically thin sample the absorbed power is $P_\text{abs}=P_0\,\alpha L$, where $P_0$ is the incident optical power, $\alpha$ the absorption coefficient, and $L$ an interaction length. The absorption coefficient is itself proportional to the analyte number density through $\alpha=\sigma N$, with $\sigma$ the absorption cross-section. The photoacoustic signal $S$ is therefore proportional to the product of laser power and analyte concentration,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pas-signal}
  S \;=\; K\,P_0\,\alpha \;=\; K\,P_0\,\sigma N,
\end{equation}
where the cell constant $K$ collects the transducer responsivity and the cell geometry~\cite{miklos2001}. Two consequences of \cref{eq:pas-signal} matter throughout this thesis. The first is that the technique is intrinsically zero-background, since with no absorption there is no signal. Sensitivity can then be improved simply by raising the laser power, unlike a transmission measurement that has to resolve a small change against a large background. The second is that, because $S$ is proportional to $N$, calibrating the signal against known concentrations gives a direct quantitative measurement route, which is used in \cref{ch:results}.

These two points already hint at why PAS is attractive next to other ways of measuring a gas. In direct absorption spectroscopy the analyte appears as a small dip in a large transmitted intensity, so the detection limit is set by how well that large background can be stabilized and by the available optical path length. PAS instead measures a signal that grows from zero, so weak absorbers can be picked up without a long path and the signal can be raised simply by using more laser power. Compared with non-optical gas sensors such as electrochemical cells, catalytic pellistors, or metal-oxide sensors~\needcite{review of conventional (non-optical) gas-sensor technologies}, the laser-based approach adds high molecular selectivity, a fast response, and no consumable parts, at the price of a more elaborate optical system~\cite{miklos2001}.

Conventionally the transducer is a sensitive microphone placed in a gas cell whose dimensions form an acoustic resonator at $f$ and amplify the signal~\cite{miklos2001}. This approach is limited by the microphone's sensitivity to environmental acoustic noise and by the relatively large cell volume it needs. The quartz-enhanced variant described next removes both limitations by replacing the microphone with a sharply resonant quartz tuning fork.

\section{Quartz-Enhanced Photoacoustic Spectroscopy (QEPAS)}
\label{sec:qepas}

Quartz-enhanced photoacoustic spectroscopy (QEPAS) keeps the photoacoustic principle but changes the transducer. In place of a microphone it uses a quartz tuning fork (QTF), the same kind of millimetre-scale piezoelectric resonator that keeps time in a quartz wristwatch~\cite{kosterev2002}.

The fork works because quartz is piezoelectric. Mechanical strain in the crystal produces an electric charge on its surfaces, and conversely an applied voltage produces strain. The acoustic wave generated by the gas pushes on the two prongs and drives them into their fundamental flexural mode, in which the prongs bend towards and away from each other. This bending strains the quartz, and the piezoelectric effect turns the motion into an electric charge that appears as a small current at the fork electrodes, which a transimpedance preamplifier converts into a voltage for the detection electronics. Only this antisymmetric motion, where the two prongs move in opposite directions, produces a net charge, so a uniform pressure that pushes both prongs the same way, as distant environmental noise tends to do, generates almost no signal. This symmetry is the root of the fork's strong intrinsic immunity to acoustic noise~\needcite{piezoelectric quartz tuning fork operation and flexural mode shapes in QEPAS}.

Electrically the fork behaves like a high-$Q$ series RLC resonant circuit~\needcite{electrical equivalent-circuit model of a quartz tuning fork}, in which the inductance, capacitance, and resistance stand for the effective mass, the stiffness, and the losses of the mechanical resonator. It responds strongly only to excitation close to its resonance frequency $f_0$. Early QEPAS work used the standard \SI{32.768}{\kilo\hertz} quartz watch fork, whereas the module in this work uses a custom lower-frequency fork near \SI{12}{\kilo\hertz}, a design that lengthens the energy-accumulation time and has become common in recent sensors~\cite{patimisco2014, lin2023}.

The sharpness of that resonance is set by the quality factor $Q$. It is defined as $2\pi$ times the energy stored in the resonator divided by the energy lost per oscillation cycle, and it equals the ratio of the resonance frequency to the width of the resonance peak, $Q = f_0/\Delta f$. Because crystalline quartz has very low internal losses, and because damping by the surrounding gas drops as the pressure is lowered, a QTF reaches a $Q$ of several thousand, far above the few tens to few hundreds of a gas-filled acoustic cell~\cite{patimisco2014}. A high $Q$ matters for three reasons. First, the resonator builds up the acoustic energy over roughly $Q$ oscillation periods before losses balance the drive, so a given absorbed power produces a much larger response than it would in a non-resonant transducer. Second, the resonance width $\Delta f = f_0/Q$ is only a few hertz wide, so the fork acts as an extremely narrow mechanical filter that answers to sound at $f_0$ and ignores the broadband acoustic noise outside that band. Third, this narrow bandwidth is equivalent to a long integration time, which lifts the signal further out of the noise. Together these properties let the bare fork work as a sensitive and naturally noise-immune detector in a volume of about a cubic millimetre between the prongs, which also keeps the required gas sample and its consumption small.

In practice the bare fork is paired with an acoustic micro-resonator, a pair of thin tubes that concentrate the sound field at the prongs and raise the signal further by an amount that depends on the tube geometry~\cite{dong2010}. The fork together with this micro-resonator and the integrated preamplifier forms the acoustic detection module (ADM) used in this work, which is described in \cref{sec:adm}.

\section{Detection Scheme: Amplitude Modulation with a Pulsed QCL}
\label{sec:detection}

The transducer responds only near its resonance, so the optical excitation has to deposit its energy at $f_0$. There are two common ways to arrange this, and they lead to two different detection schemes.

The light source in this work is a quantum cascade laser (QCL). Unlike an ordinary semiconductor laser, where light comes from an electron and a hole recombining across the material band gap, a QCL is a unipolar device in which electrons cascade down an engineered staircase of quantum-well energy levels and emit one photon at each step through intersubband transitions~\needcite{quantum cascade laser operating principle, e.g. Faist et al. 1994}. Because one injected electron can emit many photons as it descends the cascade, the device can deliver high optical power.

The decisive feature for spectroscopy is that the emission wavelength is set by the thickness of the engineered layers rather than by the material band gap. By design alone, QCLs can therefore be made to emit anywhere across the mid-infrared, the fingerprint region from about \SIrange{3}{12}{\micro\meter} where most molecules have their strongest fundamental absorption bands~\needcite{mid-infrared fingerprint region for molecular absorption}. Probing a fundamental band rather than a weak near-infrared overtone gives one to three orders of magnitude more absorption for the same concentration, which feeds straight into sensitivity. Compared with the earlier options for this region the QCL is also far more practical. It runs at room temperature with thermoelectric cooling, delivers tens to hundreds of milliwatts, and has a narrow linewidth, whereas lead-salt diode lasers needed cryogenic cooling and nonlinear sources such as difference-frequency generation or optical parametric oscillators are bulkier and more complex~\needcite{comparison of mid-infrared laser sources for gas sensing}.

The source used here is a broadly tunable external-cavity QCL, in which a diffraction grating outside the chip selects the wavelength over a wide span rather than the narrow range of a single distributed-feedback device~\needcite{external-cavity quantum cascade laser for broadband tuning}. This wide tuning is the main practical advantage for the present work. It lets one instrument reach many different absorption lines, pick a strong \nto\ line that sits clear of interfering species, and in the longer term retarget the sensor to other molecules without changing the source. The specific laser and its operating range are given in \cref{sec:qcl}.

With a continuous-wave laser the usual choice is wavelength modulation. The emission wavelength is dithered back and forth across the absorption line at half the fork frequency, $f_0/2$. Because absorption is a roughly symmetric function of wavelength, the photoacoustic signal appears mainly at the second harmonic of the dither, which is arranged to land exactly on the fork resonance $f_0$. Detecting at this harmonic moves the measurement away from low-frequency laser intensity noise and gives a clean, background-free line shape~\cite{patimisco2014}.

The quantum cascade laser used in this work runs in pulsed mode, which calls for the simpler amplitude-modulation scheme instead. A train of short current pulses at the fork resonance switches the optical power on and off at $f_0$, so the absorbed power, and with it the photoacoustic signal, is already modulated at $f_0$ and can be recovered directly at the fundamental rather than at the second harmonic. A lock-in amplifier referenced to the pulse train then extracts this signal from the noise. The detailed implementation of this scheme, including the reference path and the lock-in settings, is given in \cref{sec:am-scheme}.

Both schemes rely on a lock-in amplifier to pull the photoacoustic signal out of the noise, a method called phase-sensitive detection~\needcite{lock-in amplifier and phase-sensitive detection}. The photoacoustic signal arrives as a small sine wave at the modulation frequency $f_0$, buried under noise spread across a wide band. The lock-in multiplies this input by an internal reference at the same frequency. For an input $V_\text{sig}\cos(2\pi f_0 t + \varphi)$ and a reference $\cos(2\pi f_0 t)$, the product is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:psd}
  V_\text{sig}\cos(2\pi f_0 t + \varphi)\,\cos(2\pi f_0 t) = \tfrac{1}{2}V_\text{sig}\cos\varphi + \tfrac{1}{2}V_\text{sig}\cos(4\pi f_0 t + \varphi),
\end{equation}
a steady term proportional to the signal amplitude plus a term at twice the frequency. A low-pass filter then removes the $2f_0$ term and keeps only the slowly varying part.

The same multiplication rejects the noise. A noise component at a frequency $f$ is shifted by the mixing to a beat at $f - f_0$, so only components within a narrow band set by the low-pass filter land close enough to zero frequency to pass, and everything else averages away. The lock-in therefore acts as an extremely narrow band-pass filter centred on $f_0$, with a bandwidth fixed by the filter time constant. This is why the time constant is a central setting that trades response speed against noise, a point taken up with the figures of merit in \cref{sec:fom}.

The steady output in \cref{eq:psd} still depends on the phase $\varphi$ between the signal and the reference, and it falls to zero when the two are \ang{90} apart. A modern lock-in removes this dependence by demodulating against two references \ang{90} apart at the same time, which yields the in-phase component $X$ and the quadrature component $Y$ (\cref{fig:psd}). From these it forms the magnitude $R = \sqrt{X^2 + Y^2}$ and the phase $\theta = \arctan(Y/X)$, where $R$ no longer depends on the signal phase. The measurements in this work use this magnitude ($R$--$\theta$) mode, which removes the need to hold a fixed phase between the photoacoustic signal and the reference.

To record a spectrum the laser wavelength is tuned slowly across the line while the modulation at $f_0$ continues, which traces out the absorption feature point by point. This slow tuning is done through the laser temperature and drive current.

Pulsed operation is convenient but it has a cost. The strong current pulse heats the laser chip within each pulse, so the emission wavelength drifts during the pulse and the effective linewidth is broader than in continuous-wave operation~\needcite{intrapulse heating and spectral broadening in pulsed QCLs}. The low duty cycle also means that the average optical power is only a small fraction of the peak power, which limits the average power available to drive the photoacoustic signal. In short, pulsed amplitude modulation trades some spectral resolution and average power for the simplicity of a pulsed source and single-frequency detection.

\section{Figures of Merit}
\label{sec:fom}

To compare the sensor with other instruments the raw signal has to be reduced to a few standard figures of merit. The same quantities are used in \cref{ch:results} to report the performance of this sensor.

The most basic figure is the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), the ratio of the demodulated photoacoustic signal to the standard deviation of the noise measured under the same conditions. The minimum detectable concentration follows from it as the analyte concentration that would give an SNR of one, obtained from a calibration of signal against known concentrations together with the measured noise.

The noise against which the signal competes has several origins, but in a well-built QEPAS sensor the fundamental floor is the thermal, or Johnson, noise of the tuning fork itself, the random mechanical agitation of the prongs at finite temperature. A sensor that reaches this floor is called thermal-noise-limited, and pushing the detection limit lower then means raising the signal rather than fighting excess noise~\needcite{thermal (Johnson) noise limit of the quartz tuning fork in QEPAS}. In practice further noise comes from laser intensity fluctuations, stray background absorption at the windows, and electrical pickup, and part of the experimental work is to bring the system close to the thermal-noise limit.

A bare SNR or detection limit is hard to compare across systems that use different lasers and time constants, because the signal scales with the available optical power and with the detection bandwidth. The normalized noise-equivalent absorption (NNEA) removes this dependence. It is the smallest absorption coefficient the system could detect at an SNR of one, normalized to the optical power and to the detection bandwidth, and it is quoted in units of $\mathrm{cm^{-1}\,W\,Hz^{-1/2}}$~\cite{patimisco2014}. A lower NNEA means a more sensitive system, and because it factors out laser power and bandwidth it is the standard way to benchmark QEPAS sensors against each other.

Averaging the output for longer reduces random noise, but only up to the point where slow drift starts to take over. The Allan deviation is used to find this limit. Computing the deviation of the averaged signal as a function of integration time shows the longest useful averaging time and the best stability the sensor can reach~\cite{ma2013}~\needcite{Allan-variance method for assessing spectrometer stability (Werle et al. 1993)}. This fixes the trade-off between measurement speed and detection limit, and it is reported for the present sensor in \cref{ch:results}.

\section{Spectroscopy of Nitrous Oxide}
\label{sec:n2o-spectroscopy}

Choosing which absorption line to target is a practical design decision rather than a spectroscopy study, and it is set by two things, the lines the analyte offers and the wavelengths the laser can reach. \nto\ absorbs most strongly on its $\nu_3$ fundamental near \SI{4.5}{\micro\meter}, but the tunable quantum cascade laser used in this work (Daylight Solutions MIRcat-2300) covers \SIrange{1111}{2000}{\per\centi\meter}, or about \SIrange{5}{9}{\micro\meter}, and does not reach that band. The accessible feature is instead the weaker $\nu_1$ fundamental near \SI{7.7}{\micro\meter}. The line used for detection is at \SI{1297.8}{\per\centi\meter}, with a second line at \SI{1271.5}{\per\centi\meter} kept as an alternative. Line strengths and positions are taken from the HITRAN database~\needcite{the HITRAN molecular spectroscopic database}. The line strength sets how much signal a given concentration produces, while the gas pressure broadens the line into a roughly Lorentzian shape and lowers its peak height, which is one reason the chamber pressure is a parameter worth optimising~\needcite{pressure broadening and the Lorentzian line shape of molecular transitions}.

The same database also shows the main difficulty in this region, which is water vapour. \water\ has many absorption lines across the \SIrange{1111}{2000}{\per\centi\meter} window, and some of them sit close enough to the \nto\ lines to interfere, as the measurements in \cref{ch:results} confirm. The nitrogen used as the buffer gas does not absorb here, so the practical constraint on line selection is to find a strong \nto\ line that also sits clear of the nearby \water\ features.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

QEPAS has grown into a mature gas-sensing platform since it was introduced, and it has been demonstrated for a wide range of species and applications, as collected in several reviews~\cite{patimisco2014, sampaolo2022}. A good deal of the progress has come from the transducer itself, where custom quartz tuning forks designed for spectroscopy have replaced the original watch fork and improved both the resonance properties and the achievable sensitivity~\cite{lin2023}.

For \nto, QCL-based QEPAS has reached parts-per-billion levels. Ma et al.\ detected \nto\ at \SI{4.61}{\micro\meter} with a continuous-wave DFB-QCL and reported a minimum detection limit of about \SI{23}{\ppb}~\cite{ma2013}. Other recent sensors have pushed sensitivity further with custom forks and acoustic micro-resonators, such as a methane sensor reaching about \SI{1.3}{\ppb} with a purpose-built low-frequency fork~\cite{chen2025}. The detection scheme has diversified as well, with beat-frequency QEPAS allowing fast and calibration-free monitoring by reading the fork parameters and the gas concentration together from the fork's transient response~\cite{wu2017}, and multiheterodyne dual-comb QEPAS extending the method toward broadband, wavelength-independent detection~\cite{wang2024}.

The sensor described in this thesis sits within this body of work but is deliberately simpler. It uses a pulsed QCL with amplitude modulation and 1f detection rather than a continuous-wave source with 2f detection, and it works on the $\nu_1$ band of \nto\ near \SI{7.7}{\micro\meter} that falls inside the tuning range of the available laser. The aim is not to beat the best reported detection limits but to build and fully characterize a working pulsed-QCL QEPAS platform that can later be retargeted to trace volatile organic compounds, the longer-term goal set out in \cref{ch:introduction}.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Nitrous oxide (\nto) is one of the most important long-lived greenhouse gases.
Its global-warming potential is roughly \num{270} times that of \cotwo\ over a
100-year horizon, and it is currently among the dominant
ozone-depleting gases emitted by human activity. \todo{Add exact GWP figure and
cite (e.g.\ IPCC AR6) and the dominant-ODS claim (Ravishankara \emph{et al.}, 2009).}
The main anthropogenic sources are agriculture and soil management, combustion,
and industrial processes, while \nto\ also appears in medical and breath-analysis
contexts. Monitoring \nto\ at trace (sub-ppm) levels therefore matters for
climate science, environmental regulation, and health.

Detecting such low concentrations demands sensors that are sensitive, selective,
fast, and ideally compact. Laser absorption spectroscopy meets these needs, and
among its variants \emph{quartz-enhanced photoacoustic spectroscopy} (QEPAS)
is particularly attractive: it uses a millimetre-scale quartz tuning fork (QTF)
as a sharply resonant acoustic transducer instead of a microphone, giving an
immunity to environmental acoustic noise, a very small required gas volume, and
a wide dynamic range~\cite{kosterev2002, patimisco2014}. Combined with a
quantum cascade laser (QCL) emitting on a strong \nto\ absorption band in the
mid-infrared, QEPAS is well suited to compact \nto\ sensing.

The broader aim of this line of work is the optical detection of trace volatile
organic compounds (VOCs), such as those emitted by plants. \nto\ is used here
as a well-characterized reference analyte: it has strong, well-documented
absorption lines that make it ideal for building and benchmarking the sensor
before moving to more demanding targets.

\section{Problem Statement and Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

This thesis concerns the design, construction, and experimental characterization
of a QCL-based QEPAS sensor, operated with a \emph{pulsed} QCL in
amplitude-modulation mode, and its validation on \nto. The specific objectives
are to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item assemble and align the optical, acoustic, and electronic subsystems of
        the QEPAS sensor;
  \item characterize the key components --- the quartz tuning fork / acoustic
        detection module (ADM), the lock-in detection chain, and the pulsed QCL;
  \item establish an optical power budget for the beam-delivery path;
  \item select a suitable \nto\ absorption line and demonstrate detection,
        including a concentration calibration; and
  \item quantify the sensor's performance: sensitivity (normalized noise-equivalent
        absorption), minimum detectable concentration, and stability via
        Allan-deviation analysis.
\end{itemize}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are the following.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A complete pulsed-QCL QEPAS sensor, designed and integrated from a
        tunable quantum cascade laser, a quartz tuning fork detection module, and
        a lock-in detection chain into a single working instrument.
  \item An amplitude-modulation detection scheme for a pulsed QCL, together with
        the external-trigger and reference arrangement that lets the lock-in
        recover the signal reliably, including a fix for the loss of lock at
        short pulse widths.
  \item A full experimental characterization of the instrument, covering the
        tuning-fork resonance, the lock-in chain, the laser power and tuning, and
        a wavelength-specific optical power budget for the beam delivery.
  \item A demonstrated \nto\ detection that validates the complete instrument end
        to end and provides the foundation for future trace-VOC detection.
\end{itemize}
